Sooner or later, everyone doing their own link building runs into this. Someone offers a link swap, or you notice that a site you link to has linked back, and you start wondering whether Google will hold it against you. Most answers online boil down to "it depends," which doesn't help much when you have to reply to that email.

So this guide does three things: it shows what Google has actually said, with dates and sources; it gives you a five-question test you can run on any link; and it walks through how B2B teams can work with relevant sites without stepping on the line.

<Callout title="The short answer">

- **Reciprocal links are normal.** Most sites that get search traffic have some, and Google has called two sites linking to each other "perfectly fine."
- **The deal is the problem.** Google's spam policy names "excessive link exchanges": links that exist because of a "link to me and I'll link to you" arrangement.
- **Exchanged links usually get ignored, not punished.** A manual action becomes possible when exchanges make up most of your backlinks.
- **The safe version:** a few links with relevant peers, placed where they help readers, that you'd add even if they never linked back.

</Callout>

## What is a reciprocal link?

A reciprocal link is when two sites link to each other: site A links to site B, and site B links back to site A. The term describes the pattern, not how it came about.

In B2B it happens constantly without anyone planning it. A CRM links to the email tool it integrates with, and the email tool's integration docs link back. A SaaS company cites an industry report, and months later the report's author features that company as a case study. Nobody traded anything.

### Natural reciprocity vs. a link exchange

The same two links can be harmless or a problem depending on why they exist. If each link is there because it helps that site's readers, that's natural reciprocity. If the links are there because the two sites agreed to swap, it's a link exchange, and that's the pattern Google's spam policy is about.

Google can't read your inbox, but it can see patterns: the same partners again and again, links that appear in pairs, pages built to hold swapped links. That's why the intent behind a link matters even though nobody can see it directly.

## Are reciprocal links bad for SEO?

Not by themselves. But "not bad" isn't the same as "useful," and what happens to an exchanged link is worth understanding.

### Exchanged links get ignored, not "penalized"

Google's stated default is to try to ignore links it sees as part of an exchange. They don't hurt you, but they don't count either.

A manual action is the exception, for sites where exchanges make up a large share of their links. If that happens, you'll find it in Search Console under **Security & Manual Actions → Manual actions**, usually as "Unnatural links to your site." The fix is to remove or qualify the offending links and file a reconsideration request. Most sites never see one.

### What the data says (and how old it is)

The most cited data is Ahrefs' reciprocal links study, last updated in 2020. Among more than 140,000 sites with at least 10,000 monthly organic visits, **73.6% had reciprocal links**. Among 112,440 pages ranking in Google's top 10, **43.7% had some**.

Two caveats that most articles skip. First, the data predates 2020. Second, it shows correlation: sites that rank tend to have reciprocal links, which mostly tells you reciprocity is a normal byproduct of being an active site. It doesn't show that swapping links helped those sites rank. The study itself flags the survivorship bias.

### The real cost is wasted effort

If Google ignores links that exist because of a swap, then the hours spent finding partners and negotiating placements are what you actually lose. That's the honest reason to keep exchanges rare and selective. It isn't fear of a penalty. It's that an ignored link is worth exactly nothing.

## How many reciprocal links is too many?

Google doesn't publish a number, and anyone quoting a "safe percentage" is guessing. (You'll see "under 10%" on some blogs. It's a rule of thumb, not Google guidance.) What Google has said is directional: exchanges shouldn't make up most of your links.

In practice, these raise your risk more than any count does:

- **Exchanges are your main source of new links.** If you stopped swapping, would you still be getting links?
- **A sudden jump.** You normally pick up a handful of links a month, then dozens of reciprocal ones show up at once.
- **Partners outside your niche,** or sites with no real audience.
- **The same exact-match anchor text** on every swap.
- **Sitewide placements:** footers, sidebars, blogrolls, and "partners" pages.

## Are 3-way (ABC) link exchanges safer?

A three-way (or ABC) exchange tries to hide the swap. Instead of B linking straight back to A, the owner of B and C gives A a link from site B and asks A to link to site C. There's no A ↔ B pair to spot, so the thinking goes that Google can't see the trade.

It isn't safer, for three reasons:

1. **It's still a deal.** The spam policy is about links that exist to manipulate rankings, not about the shape of the link graph.
2. **The disguise is the tell.** You'd never need to hide a link you'd happily place anyway.
3. **You usually get the worse end.** The "C" site offered in these deals is often a satellite site with little traffic of its own.

Google hasn't published guidance on three-way exchanges specifically, so be wary of anyone who claims to know exactly how they're detected. The safe assumption is that they're treated like any other exchange.

## How to exchange links safely (the B2B way)

### Pick partners you'd recommend anyway

Before any link goes either way, check the other site the way you'd check a vendor:

- **Same or adjacent B2B niche.** Their readers should plausibly be yours.
- **A real audience.** Organic traffic that's stable or growing (free versions of Ahrefs or Semrush will show the trend), not just a high Domain Rating.
- **Pages that rank for something relevant,** not a site that exists to sell links.
- **No "write for us, link exchange available" pages,** and no outbound links to casino, loan, or CBD sites.
- **A real team behind it:** named authors, an actual product, a company page.

### Place links editorially

Put the link in the body of a page where it helps the reader, with anchor text that fits the sentence. Brand names and plain descriptions are fine; the same keyword-rich anchor on every link isn't. Never use footers, sidebars, or a page of partner links.

### Keep it occasional

Treat links with peers as a supplement to links you earn with content, data, and relationships. If swaps start becoming your link building strategy, you're back in the pattern Google warns about.

### When to use nofollow or sponsored

If any condition or compensation is attached to a link, qualify it. Google's documentation recommends `rel="sponsored"` for paid placements and `rel="nofollow"` when no other value applies:

### Where B2B reciprocity happens naturally

The best reciprocal links are the ones nobody had to negotiate:

- **Integration partners.** Each side documents the integration and links to the other.
- **Customers and case studies.** You publish their story; they mention the tool they use.
- **Co-marketing.** Joint webinars, reports, and templates credit both companies.
- **Podcasts and guest appearances.** The show links to the guest, and the guest links to the episode.
- **Expert quotes.** Answering journalist and writer requests through sites like [HARO](/alternatives/haro) or [Help a B2B Writer](/alternatives/help-a-b2b-writer) earns links without a swap.

## How to ask without sounding like link spam

Most link exchange emails say some version of "I'll link to you if you link to me." That's almost word for word the example Google's spam policy uses for link spam, and it's also why most of those emails get deleted.

A better pattern: lead with something useful you've already done, and don't make anything conditional.

<EmailTemplate
  subject="Cited your [topic] guide in our post on [topic]"
  body={`Hi [First name],

I'm [your name] from [company]. We just published [article title] and linked to your [page name] in the section on [subtopic], because it's the clearest explanation of [X] we found: [URL]

No strings attached. If you ever write about [related topic], our [resource] covers [specific angle] and might be useful for your readers: [URL]

Either way, thanks for putting the [page name] together.

[Your name]`}
/>

Why it works: the link you gave isn't conditional, so there's no deal. The suggestion is specific and relevant, so it's easy to judge on its merits. And it's easy to ignore, which makes people far more likely to actually read it.

Red flags in the requests you receive:

- A generic greeting and "your amazing site," sent from a Gmail address
- Their Domain Rating up front instead of a specific page
- An offer to pay, or to link "from our partner site" (that's a three-way exchange)
- A request for a homepage, footer, or "resources" page link
- A target page that has nothing to do with your content

## How to audit your existing reciprocal links

You can do this with free tools in under an hour for most small B2B sites:

1. **Export who links to you.** In Search Console, open **Links → Top linking sites** and export the list.
2. **Export who you link to.** Crawl your site (Screaming Frog's free version handles 500 URLs) and export the external links, or list them by hand if your site is small.
3. **Find the overlap.** Domains that appear in both lists are your reciprocal links.
4. **Run each one through the 5-question test.** Then keep it, qualify it with `nofollow` or `sponsored`, or remove it. Partner pages, unrelated sites, and conditional deals are the ones to remove.

What about the disavow tool? Google says most sites don't need it. Reach for it only if you have a manual action for unnatural links, or you know of a paid or scheme-built link profile you can't clean up any other way.
