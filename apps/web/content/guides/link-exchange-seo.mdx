If you do your own link building, you'll get the offer sooner or later: "I'll link to you if you link to me." Some SEOs say link exchanges are a quick win, others say they'll get you penalized, and most guides land on "it depends" without telling you what it depends on.

This guide is the practical version. It covers what a link exchange is, the forms it takes and how risky each one is, what Google has said about them, and a step-by-step process for doing them in a way that's actually worth your time.

<Callout title="The short answer">

A **link exchange** is an agreement between two or more websites to link to each other, usually to help their SEO. Google's spam policy lists _excessive_ link exchanges as link spam, but occasional links between relevant sites that genuinely help each other's readers are normal.

- **What works:** a few relevant partners, links placed in body content where they help, and no conditions attached.
- **What doesn't:** swaps at scale, partner pages, three-way loops and credit networks.
- **What to expect:** a handful of good links and some useful relationships. Exchanges are a supplement to your link building, not a strategy.

</Callout>

## What is a link exchange?

A link exchange is when two sites agree to link to each other. Site A adds a link to site B, and in return, site B adds a link to site A. The goal is usually SEO: both sites want more backlinks, so they trade.

In B2B it often looks like this: a SaaS invoicing tool and an accounting blog each cite one of the other's guides, or two non-competing tools for the same audience mention each other in a relevant article.

### Link exchange vs. reciprocal link vs. link swap

People use these terms interchangeably, but they're not the same thing:

- A **reciprocal link** is the pattern: A links to B and B links to A. It can happen with no agreement at all. (We cover how Google treats that pattern in our guide to [reciprocal links](/guides/reciprocal-links).)
- A **link exchange** or **link swap** is the agreement. It doesn't always produce a reciprocal pair: in a three-way exchange, the return link comes from a different site.

The distinction matters because Google cares about why a link exists, not just which way it points.

### A brief history

Link exchanges are as old as the web. In the late 1990s, banner networks like LinkExchange let sites show each other's ads, and "links pages" full of swapped links were everywhere. Once Google started ranking pages by links, those swaps became an SEO tactic, and Google's guidelines started warning against them. The wording it uses today, "Excessive link exchanges ('Link to me and I'll link to you')," has been in its guidelines for well over a decade.

## How Google treats link exchanges

Google's spam policy lists "Excessive link exchanges ('Link to me and I'll link to you') or partner pages exclusively for the sake of cross-linking" as link spam. The key word is _excessive_: Google doesn't say every link between two sites that know each other is a problem.

John Mueller made the line clearer in a short 2019 video. When you link to a site "because of an arrangement, like you link to me and I'll link to you," Google recommends making that link `nofollow`. Natural links to sites you'd recommend anyway are fine.

In practice, that means an introduction is fine but a condition is not. Meeting a relevant site and linking to each other's best pages because they help your readers is normal. A link that only exists because the other side owes you one is what Google wants qualified.

Google has also gotten better at spotting the pattern. In December 2022, it announced that SpamBrain, its AI-based spam system, could now detect sites buying links _and_ "sites used for the purpose of passing outgoing links."

### What happens to exchanged links: ignored, or a manual action

In most cases, Google's systems simply ignore links they read as part of an exchange. You don't get a penalty, but you don't get any value either.

## Are link exchanges worth it?

Sometimes. Links are still part of how Google understands and ranks pages; its own [ranking systems guide](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/ranking-systems-guide#link-analysis) says PageRank "continues to be part of our core ranking systems." So a relevant link from a real site has value. The question is whether an exchange is a good way to get one.

### What a good exchange gets you

- **A relevant link** from a site in your niche, on a page about your topic.
- **Referral traffic**, if the page has readers who are your kind of customer.
- **A relationship.** The partner you swap one link with today is the one who invites you on their podcast, quotes you in a report, or co-hosts a webinar next year. In B2B, that's often worth more than the link.

### What it costs: the time math

Exchanges aren't free just because no money changes hands. Finding a relevant partner, checking their site, writing a pitch, agreeing on placement, and following up adds up. Digital Gratified's "conservative estimate" is [2–4 hours per successful link exchange](https://digitalgratified.com/blog/link-exchange), and since you might email 20 sites to land one or two, that's 10–20 hours of work for a couple of links.

That's the honest reason to be selective. If Google ignores a link because it reads as a swap, the hours you spent on it are simply gone. A few well-chosen exchanges with partners you'd recommend anyway are worth the time; a hundred random ones aren't.

### Where exchanges fit in a link strategy

Treat exchanges as a supplement. Most of your links should come from things people link to on their own: original data, useful tools and templates, expert quotes, and partnerships. If you stopped swapping links tomorrow and your link growth stopped too, exchanges have become your strategy, and that's the pattern Google's policy describes.

## How to do a link exchange safely, step by step

<ExchangeProcessDiagram />

### 1. Decide what you can offer

Start with your own site. Which pages could you honestly link from? A guide, a comparison page, a "tools we use" section, integration docs. And which of your pages would someone else want to cite? If the answer is "our homepage," fix that first: exchanges work best when you have a genuinely useful page to point to.

### 2. Find relevant partners

Look for sites with the same audience as you but a different product: same or adjacent B2B niche, non-competing, with readers who'd plausibly be your customers. Ignore "similar DR" as a filter; relevance matters far more. (We cover where to look [further down](#where-to-find-link-exchange-partners).)

### 3. Vet them like a vendor

Before any link goes either way, check:

- **It's a real business:** a product, pricing, a team, named authors.
- **It has real readers:** organic traffic that's stable or growing (the free versions of Ahrefs and Semrush show the trend), and pages that rank for something relevant.
- **Its outbound links are clean:** no "write for us, link exchange available" page, no sitewide footer links, no links to casino, loan, or CBD sites.
- **A real person replies,** from an email on their own domain.

Then run the specific link through our [5-question test](/guides/reciprocal-links#the-5-question-test-natural-link-or-link-scheme). The most important question: would you link to that page even if they never linked back?

### 4. Propose a specific placement

Don't send "let's exchange links." Name the exact page and section on their site where your page would help their readers, and explain why in a sentence. Suggest where you'd link to them in return, but don't make your link a condition of theirs.

Showing beats telling. People who include a screenshot or a quote of the paragraph where the link would go report noticeably more replies. For a full email you can copy, see our [outreach template](/guides/reciprocal-links#how-to-ask-without-sounding-like-link-spam).

### 5. Place links editorially

Put the link in the body of a relevant page that's indexed and already gets traffic, not on a new post nobody will visit. Use anchor text that fits the sentence: brand names and plain descriptions are fine, the same keyword-rich phrase on every link isn't. No footers, sidebars, or partner pages.

If anything about the link is conditional or paid, qualify it with `rel="nofollow"` (or `rel="sponsored"` when money, products, or services changed hands). It still sends readers your way.

### 6. Verify and track

Links disappear. Pages get redesigned, posts get pruned, and some partners quietly switch links to `nofollow` once you've checked them. A simple spreadsheet is enough:

| Column       | What to record                                         |
| ------------ | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| Partner      | Site and contact                                       |
| Their link   | The page linking to you, and the page it points to     |
| Your link    | Your page linking to them, and where it points         |
| Anchor + rel | The anchor text, and whether it's followed or nofollow |
| Date live    | When the link went up                                  |
| Last checked | When you last confirmed it's live and indexed          |

Check every few months. Open the page, inspect the link to see its `rel` attribute, and look in Search Console's **Links** report to confirm Google has picked it up.

### 7. Handle removals gracefully

If a link disappears, ask once, politely; it's often an accident. If it's gone for good, look at your own link to them. If it still helps your readers, keep it. If it only existed because of the deal, that's a sign it shouldn't have been there in the first place.

### Should you exchange links with competitors?

Usually not, and most SEOs agree: in a [2011 Search Engine Roundtable poll](https://www.seroundtable.com/link-exchange-competitors-13316.html), 50% said they'd never exchange links with a competitor and only about 20% actively did. A link to a direct competitor rarely helps your readers, and they're unlikely to place your link where it matters. Look for complementary businesses instead: tools your customers use alongside yours, agencies that serve your market, and publications your audience reads.

## How to get link exchanges when your site is new

This is the most common frustration in SEO communities: you reach out for an exchange, and the reply is "minimum DR 35 and 1,000 visits a month." When your site is new, that's a closed door.

Some ways around it:

- **Stop pitching DR.** Domain Rating is an Ahrefs metric, not something Google uses. Pitch relevance: why your page is useful to their readers.
- **Offer something besides a link.** A guest post written for their audience, a customer testimonial, a quote for an article they're writing, or a mention in your newsletter.
- **Start where links happen naturally:** integration partners, the tools you use, founder communities, and small niche blogs that are growing too.
- **Build one page worth linking to.** A useful template, calculator, or piece of original data makes every conversation easier.

## Common link exchange mistakes

- **Choosing partners by DR** instead of relevance and real traffic.
- **Using three-way swaps to "hide" the exchange.** There's still a deal, and the setup itself looks like one.
- **Partner pages, footers, and sitewide links.** Google's policy names partner pages explicitly.
- **The same exact-match anchor on every swap.**
- **Trusting a "safe percentage."** You'll see 10%, 15%, or 30% quoted. Google has never published a number.
- **Swapping again and again with the same site.**
- **Joining credit-based link networks** that place your links on sites you never vetted.
- **Making exchanges your main source of links.**
